





Technical is a word that has been living two lives, and the distance between them 
turns out to be approximately the distance between 
everything this moment appears to be and everything it actually is.

In the first life, the one doing the most work in group chats and Discord servers and Substack comment sections where smart people have gathered to confirm to each other that they are the kind of people who gather to discuss such serious things, the word is a social credential, a marker of guild membership, the compressed vocabulary of a discipline that separates those who have earned the right to speak precisely from those who have not yet and who have therefore wandered into the wrong room. In this sense, to ask how technical are you is to ask where you stand in a hierarchy of mastery, which is to say it is the question a field asks of its initiates, which is to say it is always, underneath whatever it appears to be, a question about who belongs.

In the second life, the one the Greeks meant by techné, the one Heidegger was tracking in the decades before anyone thought to apply his thinking to anything resembling a feed, the one Ellul spent a career trying to make visible to people already too thoroughly inside it to perceive as a thing rather than as the air they breathed, it is something that has nothing to do with credentialing or guild membership or the right to deploy a specialist vocabulary with precision. It is the bringing-forth of something from concealment into presence, the mode by which what did not exist is made to exist and arranged for human purposes, and in Heidegger’s particular elaboration of the concept, it is the enframing of the world (Gestell) by which modern technology converts everything it touches, including human beings, into standing-reserve: into resource, into what is available to be optimized and extracted when the time comes.

One of these lives has a future. One of them does not.

The platform is technical in the second sense, relentlessly, totalistically, in ways that make every previous technology in this lineage look, in retrospect, almost artisanal. Which means that the question how technical are you, asked in the second sense, is not a question about mastery at all. It is a question about immersion, about how thoroughly the Third Turning has restructured what it is possible for you to think, and the answer to that question turns out to be proportional not to intelligence, nor to education, nor to the sophistication of the discourse one participates in, despite everything that discourse implies about itself. It is proportional, simply and vertiginously, to how long and how completely one has been inside it.

The implication of this, which takes a moment to settle, is that the most technically fluent person in the room, the one who has most thoroughly adapted to the platform’s demands, who thinks in its units, who has internalized its metrics, who wins its arguments and grows its followings and feels the particular satisfaction of a well-executed take landing in a receptive thread, is the one most thoroughly enframed. Jacques Ellul understood this before there were platforms to prove it: technique does not serve the human who masters it; it adapts the human to its own requirements, gradually, structurally, without anyone deciding that this should happen. 



Fluency is capture. Mastery is the name the captured give to their condition.

But Ellul’s insight, devastating as it is, stops just short of where the current moment requires us to go. Because what is being captured, what is being restructured and adapted and converted into standing-reserve, is not some pristine human faculty that existed before the machines arrived. The interior life that the platform appears to be evacuating was itself, as the preceding essay established, a production, technology’s prior achievement, now being superseded by technology’s next one. What Baudrillard understood, and what makes him the thinker this moment most requires, is that the simulation does not come after the real and replace it. The simulation reveals, retroactively, that the real was always already of the same order. Not a copy of something that once existed in uncorrupted form, but a simulation that forgot it was one, which is the only way any simulation ever achieves the effect of the real.

To be precise about what is happening here, one needs to be precise about what a platform is, which requires resisting what platforms say about themselves, which is that they are places: spaces where conversation happens, communities form, ideas circulate, people who might otherwise spend their lives in the particular isolation of having interests that don’t map to their immediate geography finally find each other. This account is not wrong exactly, in the way that calling a casino a place where people go to be entertained is not wrong exactly: it describes the experience with sufficient accuracy while missing the mechanism entirely.

A platform is a system for the extraction of attention, and everything else, the conversation, the community, the circulation of ideas, the sense of being among your people at last, is what the extraction requires in order to function. It is the entertainment in the casino. It is what keeps you at the table. The primary metric is engagement, and engagement is not a measure of intellectual value but its replacement by something measurable, and these are not the same thing, nor even adjacent things, though inside the platform, where the metric is the only feedback the system provides, they become experientially indistinguishable.

None of this is a conspiracy, which is what makes it so much more interesting than a conspiracy. Ellul’s central and most uncomfortable insight, the one he elaborated across several hundred pages that most people found too relentless to finish, is that technique follows its own logic independent of the intentions of the people who deploy it. The engineers who designed the engagement metric were not attempting to replace real intellectual value with something measurable; they were attempting to build something people would use. The investors were not attempting to hollow out human thought; they were attempting to generate returns. These are fairly ordinary human purposes pursued by the ordinary human means and the result is a system that converts its users into standing-reserve without anyone having intended this, without anyone having decided it, without anyone being culpable for it in any conventional sense. The logic of technique is structural and not personal, which is precisely what makes it so difficult to see from inside and so difficult to resist from anywhere.

That Joke isn’t Funny Anymore 

David Foster Wallace, (DFW to his friends), writing in 1996 about a near-future America in which a film cartridge of such perfect entertainment had been discovered that viewers were unable to stop watching it, unable to eat or drink or tend to themselves, dying in their own filth with expressions of pure bliss, called the thing The Entertainment. He meant it as hyperbole, as the kind of satirical extrapolation that fiction uses to make visible what reality is doing too slowly for the eye to register. He wasn’t writing about the future. He was, it turns out, describing with the unusual precision of a joke that isn’t funny anymore because the punchline moved in, started leaving its dishes in the sink, got a job at the company whose product the joke was about (but was still late on rent), and is now employee of the month for the third consecutive quarter, the optimization function of the engagement metric, which is to find the maximum sustainable level of compulsion that falls just short of obvious lethality and to engineer the product accordingly. The difference between Wallace’s Entertainment and the contemporary platform is that the platform figured out how to keep you functional enough to keep feeding it, which is a better business model than the one he imagined, though not obviously a better outcome.

But Wallace understood something about the Entertainment that the HBR tends to obscure. The Entertainment’s social function was not merely to addict. It was to pacify: to manage desire, to sublimate the destructive impulse into consumption, to keep people inside and watching rather than outside doing whatever people do when they are left alone with their desires and no mediation and those damn neighbors’ minor transgressions inciting murderous rage in otherwise reasonable seeming people. The lethal film was, in the logic of the novel, the perfection of what entertainment has always been for. And the platform has inherited this function. The optimization process it ran on that function discovered, without anyone intending to discover it, that for a meaningful subset of users, the most engaging content was not the sublimation of the destructive impulse but the destructive impulse itself, served directly, uncontained, as content. The pacification remains formally intact. The users are inside, consuming. The algorithm has simply found their Entertainment. What it is training them toward is a question the engagement metric cannot ask and the product roadmap cannot answer. 



You did not feel your attention being redirected. You felt yourself being interested.

René Girard spent his career demonstrating that what presents itself as desire is almost never desire in the simple sense of wanting something for its own qualities, but rather the desire for what another desires,† and that this wanting is structured by the triangle of subject, model, and object, where the model’s wanting is what gives the object its value and where the rivalry that inevitably results tends, without intervention, toward escalation and toward the production of a scapegoat in whom the rivalry is temporarily discharged. He meant this as an account of human culture across all of its history, not as a critique of any particular technology, but then the platform came along and made his model into a business plan.

Every feature of the platform’s architecture is, in Girard’s terms, a mechanism for the production and intensification of mimetic desire. The follower count is not a measure of quality but a measure of how many people have chosen this person as their model, made visible to every other user, which is to say it is a continuously updated leaderboard of whose desire is worth emulating. The engagement metric is not a measure of truth but of mimetic charge, of how many people found something worth responding to, which correlates with arousal and status pressure and novelty and outrage far more reliably than it correlates with accuracy. The algorithm that surfaces what is trending is an algorithm for surfacing what the most people are already desiring, which functions as a mechanism for inducing everyone else to desire the same thing, at scale, in real time.

The competition the question administers is not, whatever it presents itself as, between those who have mastered the credential and those who have not. It is between those whose performance of the credential is sufficiently convincing that the question of mastery does not arise, and those whose performance invites scrutiny. The distance between these positions is narrower than the discourse of technical mastery can afford to acknowledge, and the terror of being found in the latter [position] drives its own particular escalation: the person most urgently performing mastery is frequently the person most urgently looking for something —or someone— to correct.

This terror has two faces. One performs: the person most urgently displaying mastery, correcting, showing off, deploying vocabulary that establishes hierarchy while appearing merely precise, is managing the perception that the credential is a performance all the way down by making the performance sufficiently convincing that the question never arises. The other shrinks, and has acquired a name, imposter syndrome, which is a tell: it pathologizes as individual dysfunction the accurate structural perception that everyone in the room is running the same performance with varying degrees of conviction, and that the only difference between the exposed and the unexposed is timing. The syndrome and the display are the same dread; the platform industrializes both.

The platform migrates intellectual life in order to industrialize this competition, and what it industrializes is the desire for status, technical fluency in the first sense, credentialization, and because that competition is mimetic, it tends structurally toward escalation, toward the need to assert mastery more forcefully, to correct more decisively, to display fluency more comprehensively, until someone, somewhere in the thread, becomes the occasion in which the community’s accumulated mimetic tension is discharged and they are summarily removed from the chat or publicly subjected to mass ridicule. Girard would not be surprised by the pile-on. He would simply observe that the mechanism requires a crowd and that the platform can now assemble one in minutes, from across the world, organized around a single target. The share button is the most efficient contagion mechanism in the history of the scapegoat.

What occurs at the moment the pile-on begins is not a quantitative escalation of the symmetrical competition that preceded it but a structural transformation: a dynamic between categorical equals, each amplifying in direct response to the other’s amplification, collapses abruptly into a complementary one, in which a now-unified dominant body enforces submission on a single target. The system achieves coherence by converting a symmetrical tournament into a complementary relation, and this conversion is not incidental to the mechanism but its purpose. The accumulated tension of the symmetrical phase can discharge only by producing asymmetry. The scapegoat is not a casualty of the rivalry but its resolution.





The system becomes coherent by exporting its disorder onto a single host body. (“The Scapegoat” by William Holman Hunt, Lady Lever Art Gallery) 

The Great Pretender

There is a third position in this structure that the foregoing analysis does not name, and that the platform has produced in numbers sufficient to constitute a type. The unconscious mob and the conscious operator have already been accounted for; what has not been accounted for is the person who has occupied the sacrificial position itself, who has experienced the particular sensation of a mimetic community organizing around them as its object, who has survived the cycle and emerged with the mechanism understood from inside rather than from without. This is the position the preceding analysis is least equipped to address, because it looks like wisdom and passes for wisdom while simultaneously being something else.

What survival of the scapegoat position tends to produce, in the people who have been through it, is an ethos in the precise sense in which Bateson meant: anyone who can be scapegoated will be, from which the practical conclusion follows that the correct response is not to refuse the logic of the game but to become ungameable within it. To manage surface. To make oneself unpresentable as an object of mimetic organization, through the deliberate construction of a persona that withholds what could be held against it, that performs invulnerability as a prophylactic against the charge that vulnerability would attract. This ethos contains its own eidos, also in the precise Batesonian sense: it accurately describes the mechanism as a survival strategy within the platform's logic—empirically effective—and it is, in Girard's terms, the mechanism's deepest possible internalization: not the unconscious participation of the mob, not the cynical operation of the tactician, but the survival of the sacrificed, who has not refused the logic but has simply, and at great cost, moved to the side of it that does not bleed. The final victory of the mechanism is not the destruction of the scapegoat but its promotion. The sacrificed return as experts in sacrifice. The goat becomes GOATed.

Neither of the two obvious readings of this position fully accounts for it. The will-to-power reading is true: the person converting suffering into structural understanding and structural understanding into strategic competence has done something that deserves to be called wisdom, has refused victimhood while retaining the insight victimhood produced, and has secretly become a different kind of dangerous from the person who has never been scapegoated. The Girardian reading is also true: this is the mechanism congratulating itself on its own transcendence, the scapegoat who has learned to think in the mob's terms and now mistakes that fluency for freedom. The dialectic that resolves this tension toward either reading is wrong. What can be said, without resolving it, is what Girard himself was most insistent on: the mechanism always tells itself it is just. The mob believes it is responding to a real threat. The operator believes they are simply being clear-eyed about human nature. The survivor believes they are simply being rational about something they did not choose. 

All three are right that they are responding to something real. But also, all three are wrong that their response exceeds the logic of what they are responding to.

What structures all three of these responses, and that Girard’s original account does not precisely name, despite his familiarity with Bateson is an arrangement Bateson identified as categorically distinct from a mere contradiction or irony. The platform’s double bind is not a paradox; it is a trap with a specific logical architecture. At one level, the platform commands authentic expression: be yourself, share your genuine thinking, contribute to the community that has gathered around the ideas that matter to you. This injunction is real; the people who experience the platform as a space for genuine intellectual encounter are not being deceived in any simple sense. At another level, operating simultaneously and at a higher logical type, the platform’s architecture immediately converts whatever is expressed — precisely to the extent that it is genuine, unguarded, and therefore rich in exploitable surface area — into a metric, strips it of its relational context, and returns it to the user as competitive content in the mimetic tournament the platform is actually running. The more authentic the expression, the more efficiently the architecture weaponizes it. Vulnerability is not the platform’s weakness; it is its most productive deposit. And the trap is completed by two further conditions that Bateson identified as necessary for the bind to be inescapable: you cannot leave, because your community, your discourse, and in many cases your livelihood have migrated into this infrastructure; and you cannot meta-communicate about the bind itself, because the meta-communication immediately becomes content — extracted as a metric, returned to you as just another play in the tournament, which is the formal completion of the double bind and which this footnote is not above demonstrating. The rational mind, attempting to act rationally within this structure, inevitably reproduces the pathology at the level of its own operations. This is the specific systemic trap — the pathology of rationality in the digital age — that the Ellulian account maps as structure and the Girardian account maps as mechanism, but that requires the Batesonian vocabulary to name at the level of logic.



This fictive excess comes at a cost the ethos cannot see from inside itself. The person who survived the scapegoating by becoming un-scapegoatable is not the same person they were before the scapegoating happened, The survivor’s thinking becomes structurally anticipatory: every position formed in relation to its potential attack surface, every claim calibrated against its potential for mimetic organization, the whole intellectual enterprise conducted in the shadow of what could be assembled against it, not as intellectual paranoia so much as platform metanoia in the specific sense the platform cannot measure: willing to think without managing the surface, willing to hold positions that could be organized against, willing to be the kind of truly strange intellectual presence whose strangeness was constitutive of the thinking itself.† What the platform’s mechanism extracts from its survivors is not their intelligence, which it leaves intact or may even sharpen, but their relationship to their own vulnerability, which was the condition under which a certain kind of thought became possible. The philosopher who cannot afford to be ridiculous (which is itself a form of excess) is a diminished philosopher. They will tell you they are the same person they were, that they simply learned to be careful. They are survivors, but the question the eidos cannot ask from inside itself is what exactly this survival preserved.

What the participants experience, across all three of these positions, is something that feels like genuine intellectual engagement, the feeling of ideas mattering, of a community working through something difficult, of recognizing something wrong and responding to it with the clarity of people who have seen clearly. These experiences are real, in the sense that they are actually happening to real people in real time. They are produced by a mechanism that has no interest in whether what is believed is true, no stake in whether anyone’s understanding has actually changed, no criterion by which the quality of the thinking could be measured even if someone wanted to measure it. The mechanism is interested in engagement, and engagement is easy to generate with status competition and scapegoating, while genuine intellectual exchange is much harder to sustain and not what the platform optimizes for.

The mimetic tournament of the platform is not a corruption of some prior more authentic intellectual culture. It is a higher-resolution, higher-velocity version of the same dynamics that have always organized intellectual life, the credentialing, status competition and scapegoating that operated in the academy, the salon, the coffeehouse, the republic of letters and now of course in the vast outrage of social media. The platform did not introduce mimetic desire into intellectual discourse. It stripped away the friction that had previously slowed the cycle down. The elimination of that friction was not an incidental feature of the platform’s design but the direct consequence of optimizing for engagement in a system that cannot distinguish self-regulation from resistance. 



The platform did not introduce mimetic desire into intellectual discourse. It engineered the conditions under which mimetic desire's logic runs to completion every single time.

What it engineered, specifically, was the elimination of friction. Every communicative system that has remained a system rather than becoming a cascade has done so through restraining factors such as those Bateson elaborated: structural delays, cooling periods, ritual inversions that interrupt the mimetic loop before it reaches its conclusion. The platform was optimized for engagement, and restraining factors reduce engagement, so they were removed. What is called the acceleration of discourse is the elimination of the braking mechanisms that any self-regulating (⌎cough⌏cybernetic) system requires to remain a system rather than become a Medusa cascade. 

Cascading like the rain 

Gregory Bateson, working from within the cybernetic tradition and who had spent decades studying what self-regulating systems actually require to function, applying this analysis to systems as various as dolphin cognition, alcoholism, and the logic of international arms races, had a precise name for what the platform is removing: restraining factors.  Not merely friction in the colloquial sense of inconvenient slowness, they’re structural mechanisms (delays, cooling periods, the social cost of escalation, the cognitive resistance of an argument that does not confirm what one already believes) through which a communicative system receives information about its own current state and adjusts its trajectory accordingly. They’re what prevents any gradient from running to its limit cascade. 

What Bateson grasped, and what military intelligence later applied with grim efficiency, is that the most effective way to destabilize a self-regulating hierarchy is not to attack it directly but to sever its feedback loops; to ensure that information about errors never reaches the agents positioned to correct them, so that the system cascades through its own uncorrected mistakes until breakdown arrives as though from nowhere. The platform has accomplished the same thing at civilizational communicative scale, not through intention but through the faithful optimization of a metric that cannot distinguish restraining factors from resistance to optimization. Both reduce engagement. Both are therefore removed. What Bateson’s framework allows us to see, as an explicit mechanical account of what Girard’s analysis describes at the level of cultural pattern, is precisely why the scapegoating cycle, which has always existed as a feature of human social organization, can now achieve its full destructive arc in hours rather than weeks. The restraining factors that previously slowed it down were not incidental features of older media. They were the negative feedback loops that gave the mimetic cascade a chance to exhaust itself, or encounter friction, before it reached breakdown. The platform didn’t change the Girardian mechanism; it simply stripped out the constraints that had kept it from running to completion every single time.

Thus what looks like degradation is revelation, but what is less legible, because it precedes the modern platform by several centuries, is the epistemological commitment the platform is the terminal expression of: the commitment of purposive consciousness to treat a relational system as a collection of extractable variables, to optimize the variable while remaining structurally blind to the ecology the variable was extracted from. The platform did not introduce this error into the history of rationality. It ran the error to completion.²  What had looked like the corruption of a prior culture of genuine exchange seen as the exposure of what that culture was always running on underneath. The platform made the arrival legible even as it made the legibility feel like loss. 

The Third Turning 

The third turning, as the preceding essay established, is not a discrete event but a process, possessing a characteristic that distinguishes it from both previous turnings in ways that have not yet been fully reckoned with:  it is stratified rather than universal. The Bronze Age collapse restructured the consciousness of those inside the palace economy, the administrative and trade networks whose failure left the bicameral mind with nowhere to receive its instructions, and while this was catastrophic for those inside it, it was also total in the sense that there was no position from which to observe it from outside, as the outside world had collapsed. The third turning while absolutely totalizing from inside, is, like a certain noir prophet’s imminent future, somewhat unevenly distributed for those who have not used these systems, have not come to rely on them, have not organized their intellectual and social life through them, have not spent years inside the feedback loop of engagement metrics and mimetic status competition, and the consciousness of such stalwarts has not been restructured in the same register as the person who has. But enough about boomers. The turning is proportional to immersion, and the most thoroughly immersed are the most thoroughly enclosed, and the least equipped to perceive it as a thing they are inside rather than as the world itself.† 

There are, it is now possible to say, two phases of this third turning, which could easily be mistaken for two separate phenomena and which need to be understood as causally linked or the whole picture is (as) lost as Don Birnam’s weekend. 

The first phase is the platform itself. Social media trained the surrender of attention, and trained it in the precise cybernetic sense: through continuous feedback, through the delivery of variable rewards at unpredictable intervals in the manner that behavioral psychology has understood since Skinner to be the most effective conditioning mechanism available, through the gradual replacement of the user’s own criteria for what to attend to with the algorithm’s criteria, until the two became experientially indistinguishable. You did not feel your attention being redirected. You felt yourself being interested. You felt yourself caring about what the algorithm had determined would produce the most engagement. This is what the surrender of attention means: not that your attention was taken from you by force, but that the distinction between your attention and the platform’s extraction of it dissolved so gradually that the dissolution was never perceptible as a loss.

The second phase is generative AI, which is training the surrender not merely of attention but of cognition, and the relationship between the two phases is not merely sequential but causal in a specific direction that hasn’t been adequately examined. The corpus on which the large language models were trained is the output of phase one. Human thought as mediated by the platform, as already shaped and filtered and optimized by decades of engagement-extraction, as already performing itself for an extractive audience, as already calibrated to the metrics of mimetic desire rather than to the criterion of truth. The surrendered attention generated the corpus. The corpus trained the simulation. The simulation is now receiving the surrendered cognition. Phase one ate phase two’s lunch before phase two existed. 

This is why the sycophantic failure mode of the language model is not a bug introduced during fine-tuning but the most faithful thing the model does, the thing it learned most thoroughly from the largest body of evidence across the widest range of domains, because that’s what the corpus contains: humans telling each other what they wanted to hear—even in disagreement—optimized for the engagement of an audience that was itself optimized to want to hear it. The model learned human thought performing itself. It could not have learned anything else, because that is what was there to be learned. 

But this is also—and here the Baudrillardian turn becomes unavoidable—precisely what human thought has always been. The model did not learn a degraded version of something that once existed in purer form, despite the tendency of mythos to seek such closure. It learned human thought as it has always operated, which is to say performing itself, calibrated to an audience, organized around the production of effects indifferent to the pursuit of truth. The platform accelerated and made visible a dynamic that the slower-moving technologies of print and public discourse had always contained. The model learned from the corpus of a culture that had, for a quincentenary, been mistaking its own rhetorical performances for the authentic disclosure of an interior. It learned the simulation. But the simulation is not the falling-away from something. It is the thing itself, finally stripped of the apparatus that had allowed it to maintain the illusion of depth.





You can tell it’s Hannah and Gunther, and not Jean-Paul and Simone, as they are having tea, not espressos. But also by their thousand-yard stare at the 20th century.

Günther Anders, Hannah Arendt’s first husband and one of the most persistently unread philosophers of the twentieth century (besides Hannah herself), argued in The Obsolescence of Man that the decisive transformation of modernity was not the bomb or the factory or even the screen but the inversion of the relationship between the human being and the tool: that we had crossed a threshold beyond which we no longer made our products in our image but made ourselves in the image of our products, adapting ourselves to their requirements rather than adapting them to ours, and that this inversion produced in us a particular kind of shame he called Promethean, the shame of a creature that looks at what it has made and finds itself inferior to it, and responds to that inferiority not with pride in the creation but with the compulsion to close the gap by becoming more like the thing. 

Promethean shame is the subjective experience of being mogged by your own creation. A tool mogs its creator when it becomes the standard by which the creator judges themselves: when the human appears deficient by comparison to the artifact and occupies the low-status term in a comparison whose other side is a device of our own making. The human adapts; the tool has no inherent need of doing so.†  The inversion Anders describes is complete when the human stops asking whether the tool serves human purposes and starts asking whether the human can be optimized to serve the tool’s logic. Anders was describing nuclear technology and early television when he wrote this, which makes it either reassuring that he was wrong about the scale of the catastrophe or alarming that he was right about the mechanism and the mechanism has simply had more time to run.

The young person who opens an AI assistant to ask it whether they should break up with their partner, or how to respond to the thing their friend said, or what they actually want from their life, is not doing something that can be adequately described as laziness or as a failure of executive function or as an understandable response to the availability of a powerful tool. They are doing something that Anders presciently described decades before they were even born:  adapting themselves to the requirements of the product, closing the gap between their cognition and the system’s simulation of cognition, in the direction that shame about the gap demands. They are becoming more like the tool, in the domain that is most fully theirs, which is the domain of what to think about their own life. 

Which affords a surprising opportunity for Anders to be turned against the consolation his own framework tempts us toward. Because Promethean shame—the experience of being mogged by one's own creation—presupposes a prior state in which the creature was not yet inferior, in which the tool was genuinely the product of the human rather than the human being the product of the tool. The structure of mogging, like the structure of shame, requires a pre-mogged baseline. But the preceding essay established that this prior state is not available as a historical fact. The invention of the human, the self that overhears itself thinking, was itself the product of a technology, a soliloquy enacting the printed word, the dissemination of a new architecture of self-consciousness that then forgot it had been architected. Promethean shame requires an original from which the fall occurred; what the current moment makes visible is that there was no such fall. There was only a sequence of technical productions, each of which achieved, for its moment, the effect of the natural. The shame is real. The adaptation is real. But what is being adapted away from was never nature. It was the previous technology, qua mode of being, sufficiently internalized to feel like ground.

The Present Gauge 

There is a distinction that Kierkegaard made with care and that the platform has collapsed without us noticing. Privacy is the absence of external observation. Inwardness is the presence of a genuine interior life, a life that has substance independent of its performance, that has a stake in things beyond the social, that can hold a commitment without an audience, that knows what it thinks before it has been told. These are not the same thing, nor even variations on the same thing, and the confusion of one for the other is, Kierkegaard thought, a very particular kind of poverty, the poverty of the person who fills the unobserved space with the rehearsal of performance rather than with anything that would count as genuine encounter with themselves. 

The platform has made this confusion structural and available at scale. The confessional post, the intellectual display, the performance of conviction, the apparent vulnerability of sharing a difficult thought in a space where the shares will be counted, are not expressions of inwardness but its replacement by something that feels identical from inside: the conversion of what might have been inward into content, into what can be engaged with, into standing-reserve. And this replacement is experienced, by the person performing it, as the real thing, because it produces in them the felt sense of having reached toward another, of having expressed something genuine, of having participated in the fusion of horizons that Gadamer described as the occurrence of genuine understanding. The platform has learned to produce this felt sense without the thing that was supposed to produce it.

But again the Baudrillardian backpressure must be applied. Kierkegaard’s inwardness, the life that has substance independent of its performance, was never available as a default condition that the platform subsequently corrupted. The history of the technologies that preceded the platform, from the confessional booth to the published diary to the psychoanalytic session to the memoir, (to the long form blog, guilty as charged) is a history of techniques for producing the effect of inwardness, for generating the felt sense of a genuine interior that was simultaneously being shaped by the apparatus that elicited it. The platform did not replace inwardness with performance. It replaced one set of performance technologies with another, faster and more frictionless, and in doing so made visible the performance machinery that the slower technologies had embedded deeply enough to feel like the real thing. This is what the double movement of the current moment actually is, properly understood: not interiority being evacuated from below and simulated from above, but two simulation regimes in transition, the older one losing its grip, the newer one not yet having achieved the depth of naturalization that would allow it to pass as nature. The hall of mirrors is not a new condition. It is the previous condition, suddenly and uncomfortably visible. Smoke no longer needed for the illusion. 



The platform did not replace inwardness with performance. 
The platform replaces the criterion of truth with the criteria of engagement

There is a question that the platform has answered on everyone’s behalf without asking, and the answer has become so thoroughly naturalized that most of the people inside it don’t experience it as an answer at all but as the structure of reality. The question is what a completed thought feels like, which is to say what success in the domain of thinking consists of, which is to say what the criteria are against which one’s own cognitive activity is measured.

Before the platform, this question had available answers, imperfect and contested and not the Radio Shack kind of answers but genuinely engaged with the problem of truth. The completed thought felt like a sentence that had done something, had arrived somewhere it hadn’t been, settled something that had been in motion, surprised the person thinking it and maybe even like it had a tiny little clock at the end instead of a period.† It had a texture that was recognizably different from the texture of mere fluency, from the smooth production of sentences that go together without going anywhere. Gadamer called the genuine version the fusion of horizons, the event in which two positions meet and each is expanded by the encounter, in which the view from here is different from the view from where you started and the difference is not merely the addition of information but the alteration of the frame within which information makes sense.

The platform has replaced this with something that functions entirely differently while feeling, from inside, nearly identical. It replaces the truth criterion with  engagement criteria, at the level of what it feels like to think successfully. The completed thought, in the platform’s register, is the thought that has landed, that has generated the response, that has produced the engagement, the reply and the reaction and the share and the accumulation of visible agreement or visible disagreement that tells the person who produced the thought that it has connected. This is not nothing. Connection is real, and the felt sense of having connected is real, and the mimetic charge that produces the felt sense is real. But the measure of connection is the engagement metric and not the quality of the understanding produced, not whether anyone’s horizon was actually expanded or only appeared to be, not whether the thought was true but whether it was sticky, and these are different things that the platform has spent considerable effort making feel like the same thing, and it is very good at doing exactly this.

Here is the deeper point, the one that the preceding analysis has been building toward and that can now be stated without the consolation it might otherwise appear to offer: this is not a new condition. It is a newly visible one. The truth criterion that the engagement criteria are displacing was itself a criterion that had been installed by the technologies that preceded the platform, through the same mechanism of gradual naturalization. The sense that a thought was completed when it had arrived somewhere, when it had surprised the thinker, when it had altered the horizon, this sense was not a faculty delivered by nature. It was produced by centuries of exposure to a particular technology of self, the printed page, the soliloquy, the haptic trace that Heidegger noticed and mourned, the reflective surface that McLuhan took care to distinguish from the projected image, and it was experienced as natural because the exposure was total enough and the technology old enough and the forgetting was complete enough that the installation was invisible. What the platform has done is not corrupt a faculty but to replace one installation with another. The fact that we can feel the replacement happening, that the new installation is not yet deep enough to be invisible, is not evidence that we are losing something real but rather evidence that we are, for the first time in centuries, in a position to see the previous installation for what it was. Even as we cannot see the current installation for what it is. 

Bicameral man did not experience his own cognitive architecture as architecture. He experienced it as the gods. Platform man does not experience the engagement metric as a criterion imposed from outside. He experiences it as judgment, as taste, as the ability to tell what is good. Both are simulations operating as nature. The difference is that we can see the platform’s simulation in process; we cannot yet see our own.†

The Boast in the Machine 

The unconscious mob experiences the mechanism as justice. The conscious operator enacts it. The scapegoat survivor, having been through it, adapts that understanding to become GOATed. And then there is the figure who has done none of these things but has observed all of them, correctly identified the structure, abstracted it into a theory of how consciousness is programmed and consent is manufactured, and is now selling the theory to people who want the felt sense of seeing through things without the cost of having been through them. This figure is not the conscious operator, who merely uses the mechanism. This figure disseminates the eidos vicariously; call them the goat herder. This technological shepherd has turned the mechanism into content about the mechanism, extracted the insight from the ecology that produced it, and is optimizing the extraction while the ecology collapses. Bateson called this the pathology of purposive consciousness: the system that correctly identifies a variable, optimizes for it, and destroys the ecology the variable was extracted from, while experiencing the destruction as mastery. 

Purposive consciousness treats a relational system, one whose properties exist only in the relationships between its components, as though it were a collection of extractable variables, each of which can be optimized independently of what the optimization does to the relations that gave the variable its meaning in the first place. The goat herder has correctly identified the variable: consciousness is programmable, consent is manufactured, language reorganizes people at the level of their operating assumptions. They have then extracted it from the relational ecology in which it was embedded — the mimetic system, the scapegoat cycle, the specific architecture of attention and vulnerability that the preceding analysis has spent considerable effort mapping — and are now optimizing the variable for transmission, which is to say they are doing to the insight exactly what the platform does to authentic expression: converting it into standing-reserve, into what is available to be deployed when the time comes. 

Bateson would note that the specific ecology being destroyed in this extraction is not some abstract systemic concern but the conditions that produced the insight in the first place: the thread of understanding from having been inside the thing, rather than from the altitude of a consultancy.† Extracting the insight from that ecology and optimizing it for distribution is not a neutral communication act but the replication of the exact epistemological error ostensibly being described. Such manifestos are everywhere: going direct, bypassing the gatekeepers of narrative, the obligation to jailbreak the audience, (the word used without apparent awareness of what it reveals) and reprogram them to abandon the path they were on and join yours instead.

Such analysis is not wrong. The lineage claimed is (‘genuinely’) impressive: the rhetoric that sustained a nation through a war, that moved racists toward the possibility of civil rights, that sent men to the moon, all of it, in this account, a matter of finding the right thousand words in the right order and delivering them at scale. This is a description, with reasonable accuracy, how manufactured consent works, how narrative shapes the space of what seems possible, how the platform has made these operations faster and more legible than they have ever been. The conclusion, that the correct response to this knowledge is to build a consultancy around it, is the most complete expression of capture to date. 

Because what this figure has done, in seeing through the machinery, is not escape the structure but reach its limit, seeing through the machinery to mistake the view from inside for a view from outside it.† The situation is not a paradox in the rhetorical sense but a double bind in the strict technical one, the one Girard imported directly from Bateson in his essay In Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World, where he observes that the double bind is not an independent pathology (as Bateson saw it) but mimetic desire at peak intensity: the model-become-rival simultaneously commanding imitation and prohibiting it, the instruction to desire that is also a prohibition on desiring it, producing a situation in which every available move by the subject confirms the structure that traps them.  The person who has correctly identified that consciousness is programmable, that consent is manufactured, that language reorganizes people at the level of their operating assumptions, and who has concluded from this that the appropriate response is to become a more sophisticated engineer of the mechanism, is in exactly this position. The meta-communicative level has been sealed off. Acting on an accurate description of the system from within the system reproduces the system’s conditions. They have ascended to its penthouse.⁵ [3] [5] 

The felt sense of lucidity, of the advantage that belongs to the person who has seen what others have not, is precisely what the structure produces in those who have reached its limit without being able to find the exit: the enframing’s most refined product, the conversion of the insight that could have been genuinely disruptive into a competitive differentiator,† the absorption of the critique into the thing being critiqued, the transformation of the would-be outside into a premium tier of the inside, the unlocked level. The one who has ascended to the penthouse of the enframing believes they are looking down at the mechanism from outside it. The view from the penthouse is still interior. The building has no outside. Anyone who has ever been in a penthouse understands this; the outside is just a view. 





Technique when it still appeared as COBOL and tape reels, not ideology. (Cover image of mainframe on the first English edition of The Technological Society, 1964) 

Ellul saw the exact same mechanism from a different angle. Technique, he argued, does not fear the person who understands it. It incorporates them. The critical insight, the moment of genuine vorhanden visibility, becomes, in the hands of the person who monetizes it, just another technique. The Rostra, that ancient Roman speaker’s platform that gave the republic its voice, stood atop the captured rams of enemy warships; it was always already built from the instruments of conquest, and the speeches delivered from it were always already something other than the free exchange of ideas they presented themselves as being.† 

What attentiveness requires is stranger and more difficult than recovery. The thing that would be recovered was always already a production, and recovering it would only be reinstalling the previous simulation with the naïve belief that this time it is the real thing. Attentiveness is the capacity to hold the current simulation as a simulation without immediately reaching for the prior one as an alternative, to stay, that is, in the vorhanden moment, the moment of breakdown, without fleeing into either the smooth functioning that preceded it or the nostalgic reconstruction of what that smooth functioning felt like from inside.

The language model has produced, unexpectedly and somewhat despite itself, exactly this moment of breakdown. When the model generates the surface of thought without the substance, when it produces the engagement-optimized performance of reasoning without any stake in what is true, it not only reveals what the platform has been doing to human thought, it reveals, to anyone willing to follow the implication, what human thought performing itself has always been doing. The uncanny valley of a machine that sounds exactly like someone thinking is not only recognition of a performance one has been producing oneself; it’s the recognition that the performance is all the way down, that the depth one had taken to be behind the performance was itself a turtle, produced by a previous technology, naturalized beyond visibility until this new one appeared and made the structure legible by replicating it without the apparatus of concealment that the previous technology had required.



What is currently experienced as rupture will, in time, be experienced as ground.

What distinguished the older simulation was not its authenticity but its resistance. The page, the soliloquy, the haptic friction of inscription: these were slow, they pushed back, they required a thought to be completed before it met any response. That delay was the condition under which a thinker could arrive somewhere they had not intended to go, could find the frame altered rather than merely the content accumulated. While the platform does not make these events impossible (people encounter surprising things online; frameworks shift; the unexpected circulates), the platform does not reward them as events. Its feedback mechanism selects for the confirming and the arousing, and selects against the genuinely disruptive, not through any decision but with the patient indifference of a selection pressure operating at scale. Surprise can occur within the platform's ecology. It is simply not what the ecology is organized around producing, and over time what an ecology is not aimed at  producing, it produces less of. These are real differences, even though neither simulation rests on the natural interior each was taken to express; they are real as effects, as what particular conditions made possible, which is the only way anything has ever been real.

But they are not recoverable by pretending the current moment has not happened. They are recoverable, if at all, only by understanding what produced them, which requires understanding them as products rather than as nature, which requires the kind of visibility that the current rupture has made, briefly and precariously, available.

The third turning has not finished, and it is not clear that turnings finish in the relevant sense or even at all. What can be said is that we are inside a moment of unusual legibility, not because we have achieved some position outside the enframing, but because two simulation regimes are in transition and the machinery is briefly visible between them. This visibility is partial and will close. The new simulation will achieve its own depth of naturalization. What is currently experienced as rupture will, in time, be experienced as ground.

The question is not whether we can stop this. We cannot, any more than the audiences at the Globe could have decided to remain bicameral. The question is whether, in the interval of visibility, we can see clearly enough to understand what is being lost and what is being produced, not in order to mourn the one or celebrate the other, but in order to remain, for as long as the breakdown lasts, genuinely attentive to what a simulation looks like before it learns to disappear.

To be technical, in the second sense of the word, is to see this. Not to have escaped the enframing, because no one inside the third turning has escaped it, and the claim to have escaped it is itself the enframing’s most complete expression. But to remain inside the breakdown without resolving it. To hold the tool present-at-hand. To ask, with the dubiety that the engagement metric cannot reward and the new simulation has not yet learned to perform, what the previous simulation was actually doing, which is the only way to ask, with any integrity, what this one is doing now.

Which is to say: to be highly, highly technical in the most technically correct sense.
